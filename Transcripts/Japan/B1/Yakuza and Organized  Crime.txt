Sam: That sounds terrifying to most people. They do it when they make a serious mistake or fail their leader. It is a way to show they are truly sorry. By losing a piece of a finger, the person becomes less effective at holding a sword or a weapon, which means they must rely more on the group for protection. It is all about loyalty.

Alex: The structure of the group is also very specific. It is not just a business; it is like a family. The leader is like a father, and the members are like his children. They have a very strict hierarchy where everyone knows their place.

Sam: That is a great point, Alex. And this leads us to how they fit into Japanese society. This is where they are very different from groups like the Italian Mafia or drug groups in other countries. In many places, organized crime stays completely in the dark. But in Japan, these groups have been semi-legal for a long time.

Alex: It is true. They have offices in big cities with signs on the door. They even have business cards! It is strange to think of a criminal group having an official office address.

Sam: It really is. For a long time, the police and the public accepted them as a "necessary evil." They were seen as a group that kept order in the streets and handled small problems so the police didn't have to. Sometimes, they even helped the community.

Alex: I remember reading about that. During big natural disasters, like the major earthquakes in Japan, these groups were often the first ones to arrive with food, water, and blankets for the victims. They used their own trucks and money to help before the government could even get there.

Sam: They did that to improve their reputation. They want the public to see them as protectors, not just criminals. However, we shouldn't forget that they are still involved in illegal things like gambling, drugs, and protecting businesses for money.
