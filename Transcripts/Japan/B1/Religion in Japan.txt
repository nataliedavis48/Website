Alex: Hi everyone, and welcome back to our podcast. Today, we are traveling mentally to East Asia to talk about a very interesting topic: religion in Japan.

Sam: This is a great topic, Alex. I think many people are confused when they visit Japan. They see beautiful red gates and also big statues of Buddha. They wonder, are the Japanese people Buddhists or Shintoists?

Alex: That is the big question! The answer is actually both, and sometimes neither. If you ask a Japanese person, "Are you religious?" about sixty to seventy percent will probably say "no." 

Sam: That sounds surprising because there are shrines and temples on almost every street corner in Tokyo or Kyoto.

Alex: Exactly. In Japan, religion is often seen as a set of traditions and customs rather than a strict set of rules or a single belief. Most people practice what we call "syncretism," which means they mix different religions together.

Sam: I’ve heard a famous saying about this. Something like, "Born Shinto, die Buddhist."

Alex: That’s the one! It perfectly describes the Japanese way of life. Shinto is the native religion of Japan. It is all about nature and spirits called "Kami." People go to Shinto shrines to celebrate life. For example, parents take their newborn babies to a shrine, and many people have Shinto-style weddings.

Sam: And what about Buddhism? How does that fit in?

Alex: Buddhism came to Japan from China and Korea many centuries ago. While Shinto focuses on this life and nature, Buddhism in Japan is often associated with the afterlife. This is why most funerals in Japan are Buddhist. 

Sam: So, they use Shinto for the happy, "living" parts of life and Buddhism for the "ending" parts?

Alex: Generally, yes. But it’s not just about those two. You mentioned the red gates earlier, Sam. Those are called "Torii" gates, and they mark the entrance to a Shinto shrine. If you see a large bell or a statue of a figure sitting in meditation, you are probably at a Buddhist temple.

Sam: I also noticed that many Japanese people celebrate Christmas and have Western-style weddings in churches. Does that mean Christianity is popular there too?

Alex: Christianity is actually a minority religion, with only about one percent of the population being Christian. However, Japanese people love the "style" of Christian weddings. They like the white dresses and the romantic atmosphere. Again, it’s more about the tradition and the feeling than the actual religious doctrine.

Sam: It seems like religion in Japan is very flexible. Do people pray every day?

Alex: Not usually in the way Westerners might imagine. But they do participate in "Matsuri," which are local festivals. During these festivals, the whole community comes together to carry a portable shrine through the streets. It’s very loud, colorful, and fun.

Sam: It sounds like religion is more of a social glue that keeps the community together. 

Alex: That’s a perfect way to put it, Sam. It’s about harmony. People also buy "Omamori," which are small lucky charms, at shrines and temples. They hang them on their bags for protection or to help them pass school exams.

Sam: So, to answer our original question: Are the Japanese Buddhists or Shintoists?

Alex: They are a unique blend of both. They respect the spirits of nature through Shinto and find peace and honor their ancestors through Buddhism. It’s a peaceful co-existence that has lasted for over a thousand years.

Sam: I think we can all learn something from that harmony. Thanks for explaining it, Alex!
