Alex: Hello everyone! Welcome back to our podcast. Today, we are traveling to East Asia to talk about a very famous topic: the company culture in Japan.

Sam: It is a very interesting subject, Alex. Many people think of Japan as a place where everyone works from early morning until very late at night.

Alex: That is the image many of us have in our heads. We see photos of people sleeping on trains in their suits. But is it really true? Why do they work so hard?

Sam: Well, it starts with a special concept: loyalty. In Japan, many people feel a very strong connection to their company. They often call the typical office worker a "Salaryman."

Alex: I have heard that name many times. Does it mean they only care about their salary?

Sam: Not exactly. It is more about being part of a group. For many, the company is like a second family. They want to work hard so the company succeeds. There is also a lot of social pressure. If your boss and your older colleagues are still working, you might feel like you cannot go home first. It is considered a bit rude to leave while others are still busy.

Alex: That sounds very tiring. If they stay so late at the office, do they go straight home to sleep after they finish?

Sam: Actually, often they do not! This leads us to what they do after work. Have you heard of "Nomikai"?

Alex: No, I haven't. What is that?

Sam: These are drinking parties. After a long day at the office, the whole team often goes to a restaurant or a bar together. They eat snacks like yakitori and drink together for a few hours.

Alex: Is it just for fun, or is it part of the job?

Sam: It is a bit of both. It is mainly for "team building." In the office, Japanese culture is very formal. You have to be very polite and follow many rules. But at a Nomikai, people can finally relax. They talk about their problems, share personal stories, and become closer friends.

Alex: I see. So it helps the team work better the next day because they trust each other more. But what if you are tired and just want to see your real family?

Sam: That is the difficult part. Even if it is not mandatory, many workers feel they must go to show they are a "team player." If you always say no, your colleagues might think you do not care about the group.

Alex: That brings me to my next question. Do they ever take days off? I read that Japan has many public holidays.

Sam: You are right. Japan actually has more public holidays than many Western countries. They have special days like "Mountain Day" or "Marine Day." However, when it comes to personal paid vacation, it is a different story.

Alex: Do they not get many vacation days?

Sam: They get them, but many people do not use all of them. In fact, many workers only use about half of their vacation time every year.

Alex: Why would they do that? Everyone loves a holiday!

Sam: It is that feeling of guilt again. If an employee takes a whole week off, they feel bad because their coworkers have to do extra work while they are gone. They do not want to cause trouble for the team.

Alex: It seems like the "group" is always more important than the "individual" in Japan.

Sam: That is a perfect way to describe it. But I should mention that things are slowly changing.

Alex: Really? How so?

Sam: The younger generation is starting to value "work-life balance" more. They want to have time for hobbies, travel, and their families. The government is also making new laws to limit overtime and encourage people to actually take their holidays.

Alex: That is good news. It is important to work hard, but everyone needs a break to stay healthy and happy.

Sam: Exactly. Japan is trying to find a balance between its traditional hard-work culture and a modern lifestyle.

Alex: Well, that is all the time we have for today. Thank you for sharing those insights with us, Sam.
