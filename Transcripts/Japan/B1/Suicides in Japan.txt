Alex: Hello everyone, and welcome back to our podcast. Today, we are discussing a very serious and sensitive topic: the phenomenon of suicide in Japan. It is a subject that many people find confusing because Japan is such a safe, wealthy, and beautiful country.

Sam: That is true, Alex. It is a difficult conversation, but it is important to understand the reasons behind these numbers. For a long time, Japan had one of the highest suicide rates among developed nations. While the numbers have been decreasing recently, the issue remains a major social concern.

Alex: Many people ask if this is part of Japanese tradition. When we think of Japanese history, we often think of the Samurai. They had a practice called Seppuku, right?

Sam: Exactly. Seppuku was a form of ritual suicide. In the past, if a Samurai warrior lost his honor or failed his leader, he would take his own life. It was seen as a way to take responsibility and die with dignity. Because of this, suicide in Japan was historically viewed differently than in Western countries.

Alex: Right. In many Western cultures, influenced by religion, suicide was often seen as a sin or a crime. But in old Japan, it was sometimes seen as an honorable path. Does that traditional view still affect people today?

Sam: In a way, yes. Even though modern Japanese people do not follow Samurai rules, there is still a strong cultural idea of "taking responsibility." If someone fails at work or causes a problem for their family, they might feel a deep sense of shame. They sometimes feel that leaving is the only way to solve the problem.

Alex: That leads us to the modern reasons. Life in Japan today is very different from the time of the Samurai, but the pressure seems even higher. I’ve heard the term "Karoshi." Can you explain what that is?

Sam: Karoshi means "death from overwork." The modern Japanese work culture is incredibly intense. Many "salarymen" work very long hours and feel they cannot leave the office before their boss. This leads to extreme stress, exhaustion, and depression. If a person loses their job, they often feel they have lost their place in society.

Alex: It sounds like the pressure to succeed is overwhelming. I also read about social isolation, especially among young people. Some people hide in their rooms for months or even years.

Sam: Yes, those people are called Hikikomori. Social isolation is a huge factor. In Japan, there is a strong social rule to "fit in" and not bother others with your problems. This creates a "stigma" around mental health. People are often afraid to say they are depressed because they don't want to be seen as weak or as a burden to their family.

Alex: So, they suffer in silence because they don't want to cause trouble. It’s heartbreaking to think that seeking help is seen as a problem. What about the "Suicide Forest" we always hear about in the news?

Sam: You mean Aokigahara. It is a forest near Mount Fuji. It became famous globally, which is quite sad. It shows how desperate some people feel when they think they have no other options. However, it is important to note that the Japanese government is working hard to change this.

Alex: What are they doing to help?

Sam: They are opening more mental health hotlines and providing better counseling in schools and offices. They are also passing laws to limit overtime work to prevent Karoshi. The goal is to show people that it is okay to ask for help and that failure is not the end of the world.

Alex: It’s a slow process, but it’s good to know that the conversation is changing. Thank you for sharing these insights, Sam.

Sam: You’re welcome, Alex. It’s a heavy topic, but talking about it is the first step toward a solution.
