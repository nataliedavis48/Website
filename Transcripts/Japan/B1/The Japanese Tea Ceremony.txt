Sam: That sounds very different from my morning routine. Where did this tradition come from?

Alex: It started many centuries ago. It was heavily influenced by Zen Buddhism. Monks used tea to stay awake during long hours of meditation. Over time, it became a highly organized social event for the elite, and eventually, it became a symbol of Japanese culture.

Sam: I have seen pictures of tea rooms. They look very simple and empty. Why is that?

Alex: That is part of the philosophy. The tea room is designed to be a place away from the noise of everyday life. There are usually no decorations except for a simple flower arrangement or a piece of calligraphy on the wall. This helps the guests focus on the present moment.

Sam: I heard that even the way you enter the room is special.

Alex: You are right. In traditional tea houses, the entrance is very low. This means every guest must bow to enter. It shows that everyone is equal inside the tea room, no matter how rich or powerful they are outside.

Sam: I love that idea. So, what actually happens during the ceremony?

Alex: The host prepares the tea in front of the guests. They use special tools, like a bamboo whisk and a large ceramic bowl. Every movement is planned and graceful. The tea they use is Matcha, which is a bright green powder made from ground tea leaves.

Sam: Matcha is quite famous now, but I know it has a very strong, bitter taste. Do they serve food with it?

Alex: Yes, they serve small, beautiful sweets called Wagashi before the tea. The sweetness of the food balances the bitterness of the Matcha. It is all about balance.

Sam: You mentioned that there are four main principles of the tea ceremony. Can you explain them?

Alex: Of course. They are harmony, respect, purity, and tranquility. Harmony is about the relationship between people and nature. Respect is for the tools and the other guests. Purity is about cleaning the mind and the heart. And tranquility is the peace you feel after practicing the first three.

Sam: It sounds like a form of meditation. Is it still popular in modern Japan?

Alex: Very much so. Many people take classes for years to learn the perfect way to perform the ceremony. Schools often have tea ceremony clubs. It is a way for young people to connect with their history and learn discipline and manners.

Sam: There is a Japanese phrase I heard once, Ichigo Ichie. Does that relate to tea?
